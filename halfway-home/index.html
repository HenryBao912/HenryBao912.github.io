Halfway Home

Unity 6.3 · C# · Netcode for GameObjects · Coming to Steam

Solo project

My part: Game design, systems design, art direction and testing

Skills: Co-op mechanics, level design, multiplayer systems, lighting, automated testing

A two-player co-op survival game. You crash on a desert planet and get home by hauling the wreck back together one heavy part at a time, on a dwindling oxygen budget. Two players carry a single rigid body with physics replicated between them, which is the problem the rest of the game is built around.

Development story

My first plan had the ship rebuild itself piece by piece as parts went in. I turned the idea around: four parts tore off the hull in the crash, and you fit them back onto brackets on the outside. The wreck now reads as damaged rather than unfinished, which suits a crash landing better than my first plan did.

The first minute. The game opens above the crash site, finds the two of you lying beside the wreck, and hands over control as you wake. The first things on screen are the smoke from the ship and one objective: four parts were thrown from the wreck. Every clip on this page is raw capture from the editor.

Three places on the current map. The Sky Arch stands over the only gap in the east wall, on the same bearing from the wreck as the sun, so you first see it as a silhouette. The oasis, the lookout and the rest are named with one line of text the first time you see them. At the Boneyard the predator watches from the trees before it comes out; its attacks are held off for this recording.

The found story. Ten notes are left around the map by an earlier crew of two, whose lifepod had one seat. The early ones are jokes, the middle ones read like someone being left behind, and the last one, on a ledge you can only reach late in the game, says the one who stayed chose to. Notes open where you find them and are kept in a journal. The note panels are enlarged in this clip so they can be read.

Hauling together. Each grip is a physics rope from a player’s hand to the part, and the host simulates the load, so it drags behind whoever is pulling. Installing takes both players: one holds the bracket at the hull while the other walks the part in, and it seats once the two have held it for three seconds. In both clips the second player is a separate copy of the game connected over the network.

Both screens at the same moment. Left is the host, right is the second player, each running their own copy of the game. Physics runs on the host and the other machine draws what it is sent, so both players see the same core, on the same ropes, in the same place. Each machine stamped its frames with the clock time, and the two recordings were lined up afterwards. This clip was recorded on an earlier version of the map.

The rover is a salvaged buggy built on its own physics: an 800 kg rigidbody on four raycast springs, all-wheel drive, no wheel colliders, and no automatic righting if you roll it. One player drives while the other works the winch: in the second clip the cable pays out to its full 10 m and is reeled back in, turning red as it takes the strain. Cargo can also ride in the bed, where its weight sinks the suspension. The server runs the simulation and both screens follow it. The tow clip was recorded on an earlier version of the map.

Weather, building and finding your way. Sandstorms run on one server clock, so both players get the same warning and the same storm, and the wreck is the only shelter. Press B and base pieces snap to a 5 m grid; the server checks each placement and takes the cost from what you are carrying. There is no minimap: the map is a hologram over a table at the wreck that rises to eye level when you read it, with pins for whatever the crew has found.*

Leaving. Once the ship is whole both players vote to leave, or to stay a little longer, and every machine plays the same ending in step.*

*For these recordings the storm was started by hand, the hut was built by a script through the real build-mode input and plays at double speed, and the ship was set as repaired so the ending could play without hauling all four parts first.

How it’s built

The host owns the physics
My first networked test let each player simulate their own body, and the second player’s grip snapped as soon as they moved. So every rigid body now runs on the host. Each grip is a ConfigurableJoint, so two players on one part are two springs pulling on the same body, and both screens show the host’s result.
Tested against a real second player
A standalone build joins the editor as player two and plays from a script: hauling, installing a part together, driving, voting to leave. It also records screenshots and frame times from inside the build at the Steam Deck’s resolution. More than 1,080 automated tests sit underneath.
Geometry built to the layout’s numbers
The Sky Arch is a procedural mesh generated from the level layout: a 44 m span across the gap in the east wall with 14 m of open sky under its crown, standing 115° from the wreck, the same bearing as the sun. Unit tests measure the built mesh: the clearance under the crown, the clear span at the ground, and that its feet sit on the crest either side of the gap.
Parts that weigh what they say
Every part now hauls at its own mass. Two players move the Power Core at a walk; one player alone moves the Drive Assembly at about half a metre a second on flat sand, and a part left on a slope steeper than 12° slides. Both machines work out the same speed cap from the same terrain heights, so the second player isn’t pulled back by the host’s answer.
Prediction matched to the input
The second player moves on their own screen first and the host corrects them. Corrections used to be matched by tick, and under lag a grip could snap the player back. They are now matched by input sequence number: a full haul and install at 120 ms of simulated latency runs with no snaps.
An opening that froze too early
The opening shot froze both bodies before the animator had run once, so they lay on the sand in a T-pose. They now lie in an authored pose, and the same rules run on every machine, so your partner sees you kneel and stand as you wake.
A test setup that shipped
To run two players on one machine, the host was locked to the keyboard and the second player to a gamepad and the arrow keys. That split was reaching builds, where a Steam Deck host couldn’t move. Builds now give every player every local device; the split only exists in the editor.
Saves that know which crystal you took
Gatherable nodes were saved under one name per type, so while any crystal survived a save, every crystal came back on load. Each node now saves under its own scene identity, and saves record a content version so newer content isn’t mistaken for something already gathered.
When a partner drops
A disconnected player’s body is held for 90 seconds in case they rejoin. The predator and wildlife freeze while the host waits, and if the partner doesn’t come back the host chooses to continue solo or save and quit. Before this, the body stayed forever, still holding whatever it carried.
Storms on one server clock
Sandstorms run on a single server-side cycle, so both players see the same storm at the same moment: a 30-second warning, then up to two and a half minutes of 30 m visibility, faster oxygen loss in the open and wind that drags loose cargo and the rover. The wreck is the only shelter.
A fabricator that closes the loop
Crystal, plants and artifacts gathered on the planet pay for three rover upgrades at a console in the wreck: grippier treads, a winch that reels twice as fast, and a storm lamp. The server checks range, recipe and payment, and the upgrades show on the rover and are saved with it.

Three points in development. The prototype took two days and answered one question, whether hauling a heavy crate together is fun, with two capsules on one keyboard and no netcode; it also settled the game’s gravity at 0.75 g. Then the networked systems pass, then art.

Testing with Claude and MCP

The scripted playtests above run through MCP for Unity, with Claude driving the editor. They check whether the mechanics work, not the player experience.
